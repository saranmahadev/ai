Trees and Ensembles

Machine Learning1 min read

Trees turn learning into a flowchart of yes/no questions, and ensembles combine many trees into something far stronger. This district covers how a tree is grown and how it chooses its questions, why it must be held back, and the two great ways of combining trees: averaging independent ones in a forest, and adding corrective ones in sequence with boosting.

Topics on this stretch of the road:

  • Decision Trees: questions, leaves and rectangular regions.
  • Impurity and Splits: Gini, entropy and information gain.
  • Pruning and Overfitting: how big a tree should be.
  • Random Forests: many different trees voting.
  • Gradient Boosting: trees that fix each other's mistakes.

Decision Trees

A decision tree predicts by asking a chain of yes/no questions about the features, like a flowchart: "is income above 50k? is age over 30?".

Impurity and Splits

How does a tree decide which question to ask? It scores every candidate split by how much impurity it removes. A group is pure if all its members share a class and impure if the classes are mixed.

Pruning and Overfitting

Left alone, a tree keeps splitting until every leaf is pure, which means it memorises noise.

Random Forests

A single tree is unstable: change a few training points and its questions change. A random forest trains many trees on slightly different versions of the data and lets them vote.

Gradient Boosting

Boosting builds a model in rounds. Start with a crude prediction, look at what it still gets wrong (the residuals), fit a small tree to those errors, add it in, and repeat.

Machine Learning

Machine learning is the craft of building programs from examples. Instead of writing the rules, you show a model data, tell it what "good" means, and let it find the rules itself.
