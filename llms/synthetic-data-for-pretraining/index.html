Synthetic Data for Pretraining

LLMs5 min read

When real data for a domain or language is scarce, a tempting fix is to generate more of it — have a model write the text you wish you had more of. That fix has a real limit, and the failure mode has a name: model collapse.

You need: Continued Pretraining and Domain Adaptation, Diversity and Mode Collapse in Generation.

The question it answers

"What happens if a model is trained, generation after generation, increasingly on data that earlier models generated?" Each model only ever sees a finite sample of the real distribution it's approximating — never the true distribution itself. Training the next model on a finite sample of the previous model's output compounds that approximation error: each generation's estimate of the distribution's spread is itself based on a limited sample, and successive rounds of this systematically underestimate variance, narrowing the whole distribution toward its mean. This is model collapse.

Intuition

Photocopy a photocopy of a photocopy, many times over, and each successive copy loses a little more detail and contrast than the last — not because copying is intrinsically additive in its errors, but because each copy is a finite, lossy capture of the previous one, and those losses compound. Training recursively on model-generated data is the statistical version of that: each "copy" (each new model trained on the last model's output) captures a finite sample of the previous generation's distribution, and the narrowing compounds across generations.

Definition and notation

generation 0: real data, some true variance
generation g+1: trained on a FINITE sample drawn from generation g's
  output distribution; a finite sample systematically underestimates
  the true variance it was drawn from

→ repeated across generations, estimated variance shrinks each round,
  collapsing the distribution toward its mean

A worked example

A toy distribution's variance, tracked across generations of recursively re-fitting from a small (50-point) resample of the previous generation's output:

generation 0 (real data):  variance 0.522
generation 1:               variance 0.313
generation 2:               variance 0.141
generation 3:               variance 0.056
generation 4:               variance 0.018

By generation 4, the distribution's variance has collapsed to roughly 3% of its starting value — the "tails" of the original distribution (its rarer, more unusual values) have essentially vanished, leaving something that looks real at first glance but has quietly lost almost all of the diversity the original data had.

What you should notice: the collapse doesn't require anything to go visibly "wrong" at any single step — each individual re-fitting step looks reasonable, and the damage only becomes obvious by comparing generation 0 to several generations later.

Where it appears in AI

  • Model collapse is a real, published concern (Shumailov et al. and related work) for any pipeline that trains repeatedly on model-generated text, as synthetic data becomes an increasingly large fraction of what's available on the open web.
  • It's a direct reason provenance tracking of training data matters: without knowing which content is real versus model-generated, it's hard to control how much recursive training is actually happening.
  • Synthetic data isn't useless — it's a limited substitute: used carefully (mixed with real data, generated by a strong model for a narrow, verifiable purpose like code that can be checked by execution), it can still add real value without triggering collapse.

Common pitfalls

  • Assuming any amount of synthetic data causes collapse. The worked example's collapse comes from recursive, generation-after-generation training — a one-time addition of synthetic data to an otherwise real corpus is a very different (and much less risky) situation.
  • Treating model collapse as a training bug to be patched. It's a structural consequence of each generation only ever seeing a finite sample of the previous one's distribution — mitigating it means limiting recursion and preserving real data, not finding a clever training trick.
  • Assuming collapse is visible early. The worked example shows meaningful degradation only becomes obvious after several generations — a single generation's drop (0.522 to 0.313) might look like unremarkable noise on its own.

Quick check

1. What's the core mechanism behind model collapse across generations of recursive training? Each generation is trained on only a finite sample of the previous generation's output distribution, and a finite sample systematically underestimates the true variance of the distribution it was drawn from — this underestimation compounds across generations.
2. In the worked example, roughly how much of the original variance remained after 4 generations? About 3% — variance dropped from 0.522 to 0.018, a collapse to a small fraction of the starting spread.
3. Does using any synthetic data at all in pretraining cause model collapse? No — the collapse specifically comes from recursive, repeated training on model-generated data across multiple generations; a limited, one-time use of synthetic data alongside real data is a much lower-risk situation.

Key terms

  • Model collapse: the progressive narrowing of a distribution's diversity across generations of recursive training on model-generated data.
  • Synthetic data: text or other content generated by a model, used as training data for another model.
  • Recursive training: training a new model on data generated by a previous model, repeated across generations.
