Evaluator-Optimizer Loops

Agents5 min read

A first draft critiqued once gets noticeably better; critiqued a second time, it gets better still, but by less than the first round did. An evaluator-optimizer loop makes that generate-critique-improve cycle explicit: one call generates or revises, a second call evaluates the result and says what's still wrong, and the first call revises again — repeating until the evaluator is satisfied or a round limit is reached.

You need: Prompt Chaining.

The question it answers

"How do you turn a single generation into something that improves itself with feedback?" An evaluator-optimizer loop pairs two roles: an optimizer that generates or revises a candidate, and an evaluator that judges the candidate and returns specific feedback. The optimizer's next revision responds directly to that feedback, and the two alternate for a fixed number of rounds or until the evaluator passes the result — the same generate-critique-improve cycle as self-refinement, already covered on the LLMs planet, used here as a workflow pattern rather than a reasoning technique.

Intuition

A writer who hands a draft to an editor, gets back specific notes ("the ending doesn't land, the second paragraph repeats the first"), and revises accordingly improves faster than one revising blind — and the editor's second round of notes is usually shorter than the first, because most of the big problems already got fixed.

Definition and notation

loop:    generate → evaluate → feedback → revise → evaluate → feedback → revise → ...
quality after round r = 1 − 0.5^(r+1)        (the self-refinement curve from the LLMs planet)

A worked example

Quality after each evaluator-optimizer round, reusing the exact self-refinement curve already verified on the LLMs planet:

round 0 (first draft):  50.00%
round 1:                75.00%
round 2:                87.50%
round 3:                93.75%
round 4:                96.88%

Each round closes half of the remaining gap to a perfect result — round 1 closes half of the 50% that round 0 left open, round 2 closes half of what round 1 left open, and so on. The gains keep shrinking even as they keep coming, which is exactly why a loop like this needs a stopping rule rather than running forever.

Bench

Interactive bench: Generate, critique, improve, repeat. Step through evaluator-optimizer rounds and watch quality climb with diminishing returns.

Try this

  1. Step through all four rounds and watch the quality bar climb each time.
  2. Notice how much smaller the jump from round 3 to round 4 is than the jump from round 0 to round 1.
  3. Imagine the evaluator has a fixed quality bar it requires before passing a result — at what round would it likely stop this loop?

What you should notice: the loop's gains shrink round over round in exactly the same shape as the self-refinement curve on the LLMs planet — it's the identical mechanism, just run inside a workflow with an explicit evaluator role instead of a single model critiquing its own output.

Where it appears in AI

  • This is self-refinement from the LLMs planet, used as a workflow pattern. The underlying curve — each round closing half the remaining gap — is the exact same mechanism; what's new here is treating it as a structured loop with its own stopping condition, alongside the other workflow patterns on this road.
  • Verifier-Guided Agent Loops generalizes this same evaluator role into a check that runs continuously inside an autonomous agent, rather than a fixed number of workflow rounds.
  • Reflection and self-improvement, the next district, covers the agent-level version of this same critique-and-revise cycle happening across an agent's own trajectory rather than inside a fixed workflow.

Common pitfalls

  • Running the loop for a fixed large number of rounds regardless of returns. The worked example shows round 4 adds only about 3 percentage points over round 3 — past some point, another round costs more compute than the quality gain is worth.
  • Giving the evaluator no specific feedback to act on. A pass/fail verdict with no explanation of what's wrong gives the optimizer nothing concrete to revise — the loop depends on the evaluator's feedback being specific enough to act on.
  • Confusing this with the agent's own self-correction. The loop here has a fixed workflow structure with two defined roles; an agent reflecting mid-trajectory (the next district) is doing something related but less structured, with no fixed round count decided in advance.

Quick check

1. In the worked example, why does round 1 add 25 percentage points but round 4 adds only about 3? Each round closes half of whatever quality gap was still remaining after the previous round — the gap itself keeps shrinking, so each round's absolute gain shrinks along with it, even though the loop keeps making progress.
2. What two roles does an evaluator-optimizer loop need? An optimizer that generates or revises a candidate, and an evaluator that judges the candidate and returns specific feedback for the optimizer's next revision.
3. Why would an unlimited number of rounds be a bad default? Returns diminish every round, so at some point another round's compute cost exceeds the quality gain it buys — a loop without a stopping rule keeps paying for smaller and smaller improvements indefinitely.

Key terms

  • Evaluator-optimizer loop: a workflow pairing a generating/revising call with an evaluating call that gives feedback, repeated until a stopping condition.
  • Optimizer: the role that generates or revises a candidate based on feedback.
  • Evaluator: the role that judges a candidate and returns specific, actionable feedback.
