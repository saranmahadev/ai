Information Theory

Math1 min read

Information theory measures uncertainty and surprise in numbers. It began as the mathematics of sending messages efficiently, and it turned out to supply the loss functions of modern machine learning: cross-entropy trains classifiers and language models, and KL divergence measures how far one distribution is from another. This district builds them up from a single idea: rare events carry more information.

Topics on this stretch of the road:

  • Surprise and Information: measuring how unexpected an event is, in bits.
  • Entropy: the average surprise of a distribution.
  • Joint and Conditional Entropy: uncertainty about several variables and what one tells you about another.
  • Cross-Entropy: the cost of using the wrong distribution.
  • KL Divergence: how far apart two distributions are.
  • Mutual Information: how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about another.

Surprise and Information

The information (or surprise) of an event is −log₂ p bits, where p is its probability. A certain event carries no information, a fair coin flip carries one bit, and a very rare event carries a lot.

Entropy

Entropy is the average surprise of a distribution: H = −Σ p log₂ p. It measures how uncertain an outcome is before you see it.

Joint and Conditional Entropy

Joint entropy H(X, Y) is the uncertainty of two variables together, and conditional entropy H(Y | X) is the uncertainty left in Y once you know X.

Cross-Entropy

Cross-entropy is the average surprise you feel when the world follows distribution p but you predicted with distribution q: H(p, q) = −Σ p log q.

KL Divergence

The KL divergence D(p ‖ q) = Σ p log(p / q) measures how much extra surprise you incur by believing distribution q when the truth is p.

Mutual Information

Mutual information I(X; Y) measures how much knowing one variable reduces your uncertainty about the other.
